Timing: 1 minute. MaxEnt is a classic and influential presence-background method; it was not the first species distribution model. The foundational species-distribution paper is Phillips et al. 2006, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolmodel.2005.03.026. We will use elapid’s Python implementation. It shares the presence/background problem with last week’s forest, but learns the relationship in a different way. This lecture emphasizes mechanism and evaluation rather than model tuning.

The name

Maximum entropy starts with broad support

With little evidence, keep the prediction broadly spread.

Imagine four equal-area cells with temperatures of 2, 4, 6, and 8°C.

Equal weights imply a mean of 5°C.

Weights share a total of 100%. They are not occupancy probabilities for individual cells.

Timing: 1 minute. Entropy here describes how dispersed the weights are, not species diversity. In this deliberately simple geographic example, four equally available cells receive equal weight before any environmental constraints. We are showing relative mass over candidate locations, not the cloglog scores returned by elapid. This is an exact single-constraint teaching example. Source for the general principle: Phillips et al. 2006, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolmodel.2005.03.026.
Evidence constrains the prediction

The observations constrain the weights

Suppose the mean temperature at records is 6°C.

The mean constrains the weights. It does not identify a temperature optimum.

Timing: 1.5 minutes. The equal-weight distribution misses our hypothetical 6°C constraint. Putting all weight on the 6°C cell matches the mean, but assumes the other temperatures have no support. The maximum-entropy solution also matches 6°C while remaining spread across all four cells. Its calculated weights are approximately 12%, 18%, 28%, and 42%. The 8°C cell has the greatest weight: knowing a mean of 6°C alone does not identify an optimum at 6°C. These are invented cells and an analytically constrained toy calculation, not results for pickerel frogs. This simplified example has one exact constraint; actual regularized models allow approximate agreement and use transformed predictors. Source: Phillips et al. 2006, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolmodel.2005.03.026.
Intuition

The mean is a balance point

Keeping weight on cooler cells requires extra weight above 6°C to balance it.

Many weights can match the mean. MaxEnt keeps them broadly distributed while respecting that balance.

Timing: 1–1.5 minutes. Think of the mean as the balance point of a beam. Relative to 6°C, the 2°C and 4°C cells pull left. Weight at 8°C pulls right; weight exactly at 6°C does not pull either way. The rounded illustrative weights 12%, 18%, 28%, and 42% sum to 100% and have weighted mean 6°C. The balance also follows from 12 × 4 + 18 × 2 = 42 × 2. Do not require students to calculate the equation; use it to check the illustration. The mean constraint alone does not force the largest weight onto 8°C: 5%, 10%, 65%, and 20% would also average 6°C, but have lower entropy. MaxEnt selects the maximum-entropy distribution among the distributions satisfying the constraint. The exact example is approximately 11.97%, 18.20%, 27.70%, and 42.14%. Thus the illustration explains the counterbalancing, while the maximum-entropy principle determines the particular solution. Knowing a mean alone does not identify a temperature optimum. Additional evidence about spread or transformed predictors can change the response shape. These are relative weights across four equally available cells, not occupancy probabilities or confidence intervals. Source: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolmodel.2005.03.026. Two climate variables

Balance temperature and rainfall together

At recorded sites, the averages are:

6°C temperature
1,100 mm rainfall

The same weights must balance both averages.

MaxEnt spreads weight as broadly as these constraints allow.

Each circle represents a climate combination, not a map location. Circle area shows relative weight.

Timing: 1–1.5 minutes. Extend the beam analogy to balancing a flat tray: left/right is temperature, and up/down is rainfall. We now have two summaries of the occurrence records, and one set of weights has to reproduce both. Imagine four equally available cells with the climate combinations shown. Equal weights would average 5°C and 1,000 mm. The observed averages are warmer and wetter, so weight shifts toward the warm, wet corner while every combination retains some weight. The coral cross is the weighted center of all four circles; no cell needs to sit exactly at that center. The exact weights are 1/12, 1/6, 1/4, and 1/2, displayed as rounded percentages. They sum to one and give weighted means of exactly 6°C and 1,100 mm. Warm cells together carry two-thirds of the weight and wet cells carry three-quarters. For this complete four-combination grid, the maximum-entropy joint weights are the products of those marginal weights. These are normalized relative weights over equally available cells, not occupancy probabilities or evidence of a climate optimum. This teaching example constrains only two raw means, with no regularization or interaction constraint. Real landscapes may have correlated predictors and missing combinations, and real MaxEnt fits can constrain additional transformed features. Reproducible figure: assets/Lecture08-build-figures.py. Source for the maximum-entropy principle: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolmodel.2005.03.026.

Accounting for observation bias when sampling background points

The background can influence the predictions

Observations will be biased toward where humans usually go (near roads, in parks, where they think the organism will be), so it's important to replicate this bias as much as possible when sampling background points.

In the lab we will take mean annual temperature as a proxy for sampling bias, but in practice you might do something fancier.

Timing: 1 minute. The same occurrence records can imply different environmental associations against a different background distribution. A defensible bias layer should represent observation effort when the aim is to compensate for sampling bias. Favoring warm cells is a lab illustration, not evidence that this corrects amphibian sampling. The effect on both fitting and AUC depends on the comparison population. Source: https://earth-chris.github.io/elapid/module/geo/#elapid.geo.sample_bias_file and Elith et al. 2011, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1472-4642.2010.00725.x.
Geographic sample weights

Sample weights change a point’s influence

Many neighboring observations can repeat similar information.

Distance-based weights give relatively isolated records more influence.

Timing: 1 minute. This plot uses ela.distance_weights with n_neighbors=-1, meaning average distance to the other observations, calculated separately for presences and background. This is a heuristic that changes contributions to model fitting, not proof of corrected sampling or independent records. Distance calculations use EPSG:26919 in meters. Weights are not environmental predictor columns. Source: https://earth-chris.github.io/elapid/module/geo/#elapid.geo.distance_weights.
Geographically aware splitting

Geographic splits withhold whole blocks

Keep an entire square in training or testing.

Lab08 uses alternating 50 km squares.

Teal training set. Coral test set. Nearby locations often share climate and sampling history.

Timing: 1 minute. The geometry must use a projected CRS in meters before grid_size=50_000 means 50 km. The number is a teaching choice, not a measured autocorrelation distance or a universal recommendation. Ask students what would happen with much smaller squares. Keeping blocks together controls one type of leakage, but border neighbors and repeated climate conditions remain possible. Source: https://earth-chris.github.io/elapid/module/train_test_split/#elapid.train_test_split.checkerboard_split.
Evaluating robustness of test/train splits

Cross-validation rotates the test group

Presence/background points are split into 'K-fold' groups, and each group takes a turn as the withheld data. A new model is fit to each 'fold' of the data, and performance is evaluated as the average across N-folds.

Group AGroup BGroup C
Round 1TestTrainTrain
Round 2TrainTestTrain
Round 3TrainTrainTest

A group is often called a “fold.”

Timing: 1 minute. This is an illustrative three-fold design. The groups are fixed before fitting; each model excludes its test group. Cross-validation is repeated evaluation on different withheld groups, not just calling fit several times on all rows. Inspect the individual scores as well as their summary. If cross-validation is used to choose predictors/settings, an additional independent evaluation or nested design is needed to judge that selection. Source: https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/cross_validation.html.
Two-fold spatial cross-validation

The checkerboard groups can swap roles

Each model predicts the group of locations it did not use for learning.

Timing: 1 minute. Checkerboard splitting creates two complementary groups. The assignment first fits A and evaluates B, then fits a new model on B and evaluates A. It is two-fold spatial cross-validation when both directions are run. The two training sizes differ, and environmental coverage also differs, so expect potentially different scores. Keep predictors, model settings, and the original background draw fixed. Source: https://earth-chris.github.io/elapid/module/train_test_split/.
Leave-one-out (LOO) cross-validation is the logical extreme of k-fold

Buffered LOO controls for spatial auto-correlation

Hold out one observation as the test data.

Remove training points within 50 km.

Fit the SDM to all the remaining points.

Evaluate prediction accuracy for the one held-out point.

Repeat for another observation.

Timing: 1 minute. This is one actual buffered split from a seeded subset of the Maine modeling points. Distances use projected meters. Buffered leave-one-out asks about prediction away from observed sites and can be computationally expensive when repeated. One held-out presence alone cannot define ROC AUC because it supplies no comparison background group. Any evaluation combining such predictions with background scores needs to describe whether that background was used in fitting; it is not automatically equivalent to holding out both classes. Source: https://earth-chris.github.io/elapid/module/train_test_split/#elapid.train_test_split.BufferedLeaveOneOut.

Does a recorded site get the higher score?

Ranking means ordering sites from higher to lower predicted suitability.
A site scoring 0.9 ranks above a site scoring 0.7.

The recorded site scores higher in 8 of 9 pairs.
This gives AUC = 8 ÷ 9 ≈ 0.89.

AUC summarizes this ordering: 1 = every pair, 0.5 ≈ chance ordering.

Timing: 1.5 minutes. Read one table cell aloud before scanning the table: compare the recorded site scoring 0.9 with the background site scoring 0.7. The recorded site gets the higher suitability prediction. Repeat for all three backgrounds and all three recorded sites. Only the 0.6 versus 0.7 pair goes the other way. AUC summarizes the probability that a randomly selected presence receives a higher score than a randomly selected background point, giving half credit to ties. Here eight of nine equally weighted pairs succeed, so AUC is 0.89. An AUC below 0.5 means the ordering tends to go the opposite way. We are comparing presences with sampled background, not confirmed absences. Avoid calling a background prediction ecologically wrong, because background may contain occupied sites. Weighted AUC changes each comparison’s contribution; this table illustrates the unweighted case. AUC uses the order of the predictions rather than their absolute distances. Source: https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.metrics.roc_auc_score.html.

Timing: 1.5 minutes. Locate cutoff 0.8 in the previous table and then on the graph: zero background sites qualify, so x is 0%; two of three recorded sites qualify, so y is 67%. Repeat for 0.5: x is 33%, y is 100%. Moving through all possible cutoffs traces the ROC curve. With a finite set of predictions, several cutoffs may produce the same point. ROC stands for receiver operating characteristic; focus on the axes rather than memorizing the name. The diagonal represents chance ordering. The area under this curve equals the pairwise AUC introduced earlier. Percentages on the axes run from 0% to 100%, equivalent to fractions from 0 to 1; the normalized area ranges from 0 to 1. The conventional false-positive rate treats background as the negative label, but background is not confirmed absence. Source: https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/model_selection/plot_roc.html.
High AUC isn't always 'better'

Spatially/environmentally informed splits can improve model generalizability

Evaluate usingAUCWhat is the model predicting?
Training records0.875Locations it already learned from
A random test split0.798Held-out points, often near training points
A spatial test split0.664Entire withheld geographic blocks

A higher random-test AUC can reflect an easier test, rather than better prediction in new areas.

Nearby points share climate and local patterns. Spatial splits reduce this overlap and help expose overfitting to those patterns.

Timing: 2 minutes. Distinguish suitability scores at individual sites from AUC, which summarizes how well a set of those scores separates recorded and background sites. The table contains actual fitted results, not the six-point teaching example. Training AUC uses the fitting data and can be optimistic. Random holdouts can remain spatially similar to training points, allowing a model to benefit from shared local conditions or sampling patterns. Withholding geographic blocks reduces this dependence and asks a harder question about prediction in other areas. A lower spatial AUC can expose reliance on local patterns that fails to generalize, including spatial overfitting. Spatial evaluation does not itself remove overfitting from the fitted model; using spatial validation to choose predictors or model complexity can help select models that generalize. Appropriate block size matters, and adjacent checkerboard blocks do not guarantee complete independence. Spatial splits can also withhold unfamiliar climates. Thus this AUC gap alone cannot identify its cause, prove overfitting, or establish that every spatial split is better for every prediction goal.
